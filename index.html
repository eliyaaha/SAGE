SAGE: A Strategy-Aware Graph-Enhanced Generation Framework For Online Counseling

Abstract

Effective online mental health counseling is a complex, theory-driven process requiring the simultaneous integration of psychological frameworks, real-time distress signals, and strategic intervention planning. This level of clinical reasoning is critical for safety and therapeutic effectiveness but is often missing in general-purpose Large Language Models.

We introduce SAGE, a novel framework designed to bridge the gap between structured clinical knowledge and generative AI. SAGE constructs a heterogeneous graph that unifies conversational dynamics with a psychologically grounded layer, explicitly anchoring interactions in a theory-driven lexicon. Our architecture first employs a Next Strategy Classifier to identify the optimal therapeutic intervention. Subsequently, a Graph-Aware Attention mechanism projects graph-derived structural signals into soft prompts, conditioning the LLM to generate responses that maintain clinical depth. Validated through both automated metrics and expert human evaluation, SAGE outperforms baselines in strategy prediction and recommended response quality. By providing actionable intervention recommendations, SAGE serves as a cutting-edge decision-support tool designed to augment human expertise in high-stakes online crisis counseling.

Why counselors need more than a fluent reply

On a crisis hotline, a counselor often handles several chats at once. Risk signals arrive in fragments, and a help-seeker’s emotional state can shift within a few messages. To respond well, the counselor has to keep track of the conversation, notice subtle signs of risk, and choose the right clinical strategy, all under heavy cognitive load.

General-purpose LLMs can write fluent, empathic text, but they lack the clinical reasoning and psychological grounding this work needs. SAGE is built as decision support for the counselor, around two hypotheses:

  • Modeling the counselor’s decisions requires multi-layered clinical context: the session, the dialogue, and the psychological risk factors behind it.
  • An explicit intermediate clinical inference step, deciding which strategy to use before writing, makes the generated reply more clinically grounded.

A help-seeker writes

The session arrives with basic metadata such as age and gender. SAGE works at intervention points: the last help-seeker message before the counselor applies a therapeutic strategy.

A clinical psychologist annotated 2,258 intervention points across 150 real sessions from Sahar’s national crisis hotline.

The lexicon names the risk factor

Help-seeker messages are matched against the Suicide-Risk Factors lexicon: over 4,000 phrases in 20 categories, grounded in PHQ-9, the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale and the Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire.

Step 3 of 5

Choose the next strategy

The Next Strategy Classifier, a Heterogeneous Graph Transformer over the session graph, predicts which of three strategies the counselor should use next.

  • ReflectionEmpathic validation and mirroring of emotional content
  • ExplorationInviting the help-seeker to say more, including safety assessment
  • SuggestionActionable coping strategies and recommendations

Step 4 of 5

Turn graph signals into soft prompts

Graph-Aware Attention uses the current message to attend over the structural and psychological signals in the graph, and projects them into 8 virtual tokens that condition the language model.

The token names here are illustrative. The real tokens are learned vectors, not words.

Step 5 of 5

Suggest a reply

A LoRA fine-tuned LLM writes a recommended reply, conditioned on the predicted strategy and its clinical definition, the virtual tokens, and the recent dialogue. The counselor stays in charge and decides whether to use it.

The heterogeneous graph

Each session becomes one graph with three layers. A Heterogeneous Graph Transformer learns a separate attention for each relation type, so it can tell a temporal link between messages from a link to a risk factor.

Conversational

Session-wide context: the session itself, demographic metadata and its distress categories.

Utterance

The dialogue: every help-seeker and counselor message, linked in the order it was sent.

Psychological

Clinical grounding: lexicon categories that anchor help-seeker messages to known risk factors.

What a psychologist preferred

A licensed psychologist compared SAGE with a fine-tuned baseline in a blind pairwise test of 307 responses. SAGE was preferred in 50.2% of pairs and the baseline in 34.5% (p ≈ 0.006). The gap is widest early in the session, when there is little to go on.

When SAGE was preferred, the psychologist most often cited empathy (97.4%), contextual relevance (77.9%), fluency (63.6%) and safety (37.0%). Intra-rater agreement was κ = 0.91. On strategy prediction, the full graph reaches F1 0.77 and MCC 0.59, against MCC 0.12 for a text-only model.

A case where it matters

The help-seeker describes exhaustion and a lack of energy, saying that every day feels like a “war”. He shares the pain of a broken heart after being rejected. His last message:

“I ask myself every day what I’ve done in my life to deserve this. I take blow after blow, one bad thing after another. I have no strength left; sometimes I just want anything to end.”

Baseline reply

“Dear, I hear how broken your heart is… how much you are suffering… I hear how lost you feel… I am worried about you…”

Offers empathy, but does not act on the critical distress signal or assess immediate risk.

SAGE reply

“Dear, I hear the despair… the pain… the urge to stop… I am worried about you… Do you have an intention to harm yourself?”

Follows clinical safety protocol by asking directly about risk.

Contributions

  • Graph-structured clinical knowledge for crisis-support LLMs. A heterogeneous graph links the dialogue to session context and to theory-driven risk factors, and conditions generation through soft prompts.
  • Proactive risk assessment under sparse context. The graph helps most at the start of a session, where risk is hardest to judge and LLMs have the least to work with.
  • A foundation for real-world deployment. SAGE is designed as decision support that keeps the counselor in control, and sets up broader human evaluation.
